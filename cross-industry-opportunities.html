Cross-Industry Expansion Opportunities

From obvious sellers within the core trade awards, out to genuinely lateral opportunities in adjacent and unrelated industries.

01Adjacent property 02Retail & distribution 03Financial services 04Government & schemes 05Trust infrastructure 06New sister verticals 07Data & M&A 08Media & licensing

— How To Read This

The revenue inventory covers everything sellable within the trade awards itself. This document is different in kind: it asks where the underlying asset the business builds — a credible, verified list of good tradespeople, backed by real judging and real client feedback — has value to industries that have nothing to do with awards at all.

It's organised as a set of widening rings, from directly adjacent (already touched on with real estate) out to genuinely unrelated sectors and business models. Not every idea here is a year-one project — several are explicitly speculative — but they're included because "think outside the box" is the brief, and the value of a wide net is finding the two or three ideas worth taking seriously that a narrower list would never surface.

01 Directly Adjacent Property Sectors

The closest ring: same customer journey, same moment of need

Surveyors & conveyancers. Anyone renovating or buying property needs both — a natural extension of the same directory, and a natural cross-referral partner for the trades already in it.

Removals & storage. Every home move touches trades on both ends. A "moving house" bundle — removals, cleaners, locksmiths, storage — packaged together is a stronger consumer product than any one category alone.

Home staging & interior styling. Sits between trades and real estate agents — stagers are hired by both, making them a natural bridge category if a property-sector sister awards launches.

Architects & structural designers. Higher-value, lower-volume than trades, but the same trust problem — a homeowner planning an extension needs an architect before they need a builder.

02 Retail & Distribution Partnerships

Putting the directory in front of people who are already buying

DIY/hardware retailer partnerships. Large hardware chains constantly field "can you recommend someone to fit this" questions. Becoming the official in-store or online "find an installer" referral partner turns retail footfall into lead-gen revenue, paid per referral or as a fixed placement fee.

Property portal integration. Licensing the vetted-trades directory as a feature inside a property listings site ("find a surveyor/mover/renovator" widget on every listing) — a B2B2C data licensing deal, not just a sponsorship.

Manufacturer-specific certification. A product manufacturer (a boiler brand, a paint brand, a tile brand) wants installers who represent their product well. A "Certified [Brand] Installer" tier — judged partly on the manufacturer's own criteria — is a much larger, ongoing licensing relationship than a simple category sponsorship, paid per certified installer per year.

03 Financial Services Crossover

Where "verified quality" becomes "reduced risk" to someone else's business

Home insurance partnerships. Insurers care about claim risk. A homeowner using a verified, award-recognised tradesperson is plausibly lower-risk work — worth exploring as a genuine premium-discount partnership, not just an affiliate link, since it gives the insurer a real underwriting angle, not just a marketing one.

Renovation & home-improvement lending. Lenders offering renovation loans want confidence the money is being spent with a competent contractor. Becoming the preferred/recommended contractor network for a lender's loan product earns a referral fee per loan originated.

Invoice factoring & trade finance. Trades often have cash-flow problems waiting on payment. Alternative lenders targeting this market want access to a vetted, less-risky customer base — a natural lead-gen partnership.

Home warranty & appliance protection. Warranty providers need a network of vetted repair professionals to fulfil claims — the same network the awards already maintains.

04 Government, Utilities & Regulated Schemes

Arguably the single stickiest opportunity in this whole document

Government-backed home improvement schemes (insulation, heat pumps, solar, disability adaptations) almost always require work to be carried out by an approved installer. If the awards' accreditation became a recognised route onto one of these approved-installer panels, contractors would need to hold it to access government-funded work — not a nice-to-have marketing badge, a genuine commercial requirement. The same logic applies to utility companies running efficiency schemes, and to local councils maintaining approved-contractor lists for social housing repairs and adaptations.

Why this is different from everything else in this document: most revenue streams here are things a trade chooses to buy because it helps them. This one could become something a trade needs in order to access an entire category of work — a fundamentally stronger commercial position, though it requires real regulatory credibility to get there, not something to attempt in year one.

05 Trust & Consumer Protection Infrastructure

Turning the badge into a backed guarantee, not just a badge

Consumer trust schemes that come with actual financial backing (deposit protection, dispute mediation, a guarantee fund) carry far more weight than a logo alone — this is roughly the model behind government-endorsed trust schemes in several countries. A "protected work" tier, where award-winning trades opt into a small transaction levy that funds a mediation/guarantee scheme for their customers, would meaningfully differentiate the badge from every purely reputational award on the market, and creates a recurring, transaction-linked revenue stream rather than a flat annual fee.

06 Completely Different Sectors as New Sister Verticals

Same underlying problem, totally different industry

The property-sector expansion already covered shares a customer journey with trades. These don't — but they share the exact structural pattern that makes trade awards work in the first place: a fragmented market of small local businesses, a consumer who can't easily verify quality themselves, and real commercial upside for winners. Any of these could become an entirely separate sister awards brand:

Automotive repair & garages. Near-identical trust problem to trades — "is this garage honest or will they overcharge me" is the same question as "is this builder honest."

Home & elderly care services. Higher emotional stakes, strong government/council tie-in potential (similar to Ring 4), and a sector desperate for credible quality signals.

Pet services. Groomers, vets, dog walkers, boarding kennels — a genuinely large, fragmented, trust-driven consumer market with very little award infrastructure currently.

Weddings & events. Caterers, florists, photographers, venues — structurally identical to trades (one-off high-stakes purchase, hard to verify quality in advance, strong word-of-mouth culture) and a market that already has some appetite for "award-winning" branding.

07 Data, Investment & M&A Intelligence

The most lateral idea in this document

Private equity firms actively consolidate fragmented trade sectors — buying up well-run local businesses and rolling them into larger groups is a well-established investment strategy. A multi-year database of verified, performance-ranked trade businesses is close to a ready-made acquisition target list: which businesses are consistently well-reviewed, growing, well-run, and owner-operated (a common acquisition profile). Selling anonymised or opt-in deal-sourcing intelligence to private equity and investors looking to acquire in this space is a genuinely unusual revenue line for an awards business — but it's a direct, valuable use of data the business would be collecting anyway as part of running the awards, at effectively zero marginal cost to produce.

08 Media, Content & Methodology Licensing

Turning the brand itself into the product

Consumer media crossover. A "how to choose a builder you can trust" content brand — magazine, YouTube channel, podcast — built around winner stories and judging insights, monetised through advertising and sponsored content rather than awards fees directly.

Home tour events. Ticketed show-home tours of winning builders' completed projects, in the same spirit as televised home-renovation content — sponsorship and ticket revenue, with strong social content value for winners too.

Licensing the judging methodology itself. Large franchise networks (cleaning, pest control, home services franchises) often want to run their own internal "best franchisee" awards. Licensing the judging framework and technology as a white-label service to a franchisor is a B2B sale that has nothing to do with the public-facing consumer awards at all.

— Where To Actually Start

Not a ranking of best ideas — a note on which rings are reachable soonest versus which need groundwork first:

  • Reachable early, once the core awards has any credibility: manufacturer certification partnerships (Ring 2) and retail/portal referral deals (Ring 2) — both are straightforward commercial conversations that don't require regulatory relationships or new infrastructure.
  • Worth deliberately building toward: the government/utility approved-installer angle (Ring 4) and the trust/guarantee scheme (Ring 5) — the two rings with the strongest long-term defensibility, but both need the core awards to already be credible and well-governed before anyone regulatory will engage.
  • Opportunistic, low-effort to test: the data/M&A intelligence angle (Ring 7) costs almost nothing to explore once a few years of data exist — worth a few exploratory conversations with PE firms active in trade consolidation, even before committing real resource to it.
  • The big structural bet: new sister verticals in unrelated sectors (Ring 6) — each one is really a new business built on proven infrastructure, so the right moment is once the core trade awards is running smoothly enough to spare the attention, not before.
Note: several ideas here (particularly Rings 4, 5, and 7) are genuinely speculative and would need real due diligence — regulatory conversations, legal review, and direct conversations with the relevant partners — before treating them as committed strategy. They're included because they're the kind of opportunity a narrower brainstorm would miss entirely, not because they're ready to execute on.
